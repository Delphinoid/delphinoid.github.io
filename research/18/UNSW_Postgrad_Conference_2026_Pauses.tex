\section{What are Fusion Categories?}

\begin{frame}
\noindent\centerline{\LARGE\textcolor{structure}{\underline{What are Fusion Categories?}}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
\frametitle{Motivation I}
In the 1920s, it was proposed by Dirac that with the elimination of only one basic assumption, quantum mechanics would follow naturally from the classical theory. This assumption was \textcolor{structure}{commutativity} (\cite{Dir26}).\pause\\[2ex]

Since then, non-commutative mathematics has played an important role in modern physics. In fact, ``quantum'' has become almost synonymous with ``non-commutative'' on the mathematical side.\pause\\[2ex]

From this perspective, fusion categories can be viewed as a \textcolor{structure}{quantum analogue to the finite-dimensional representation theory of finite groups}.\\[2ex]

This can be made quite precise as follows. For simplicity, we will always assume to be working over an \textcolor{structure}{algebraically closed field of characteristic $0$}.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
\frametitle{Motivation II}
Consider the category $\textcat{Rep}(G)$ of finite-dimensional representations of a finite group $G$. This category has the following properties.\pause\\[1.5ex]
\begin{itemize}[<+->]
\item \textcolor{structure}{Linear}: hom-sets are vector spaces.
\item \textcolor{structure}{Semisimple}: every representation is a direct sum of irreducibles.
\item \textcolor{structure}{Finite}: there are only finitely many irreducibles.
\item \textcolor{structure}{Simple unit}: the trivial representation $\mathbbm{1}$ is irreducible.
\item \textcolor{structure}{Monoidal}: we have linear tensor products with unit $\mathbbm{1}$.
\item \textcolor{structure}{Rigid}: every representation $X$ has a contragredient dual $X^*$.
\item \textcolor{structure}{Symmetric}: the braiding $c_{X,Y}(x \otimes y) \coloneqq y \otimes x$ has inverse $c_{Y,X}$.\\[2ex]
%\item \textcolor{structure}{Positive}: every object has non-negative integer dimension.\\[2ex]
\end{itemize}\pause[\thebeamerpauses]
By Tannakian duality (\cite{Del90}, \cite{Del02}), every category satisfying these properties is equivalent (as a tensor category) to some $\textcat{Rep}(G)$!
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
\frametitle{Motivation III}
In order to ``quantize'' $\textcat{Rep}(G)$, there are two particularly interesting ways of throwing away commutativity.\pause\\[1.5ex]
\begin{itemize}[<+->]
\item We can ask that the braiding be ``as non-symmetric as possible'', giving us \textcolor{structure}{modular tensor categories}.\\[2ex]
\item More generally, we can eliminate entirely the requirement of a braiding, whence we arrive at the definition of a \textcolor{structure}{fusion category}.\\[2ex]
\end{itemize}\pause[\thebeamerpauses]
Modular tensor categories have applications in topological quantum computing, where they are used to model anyons, as well as $(2+1)$D topological quantum field theory and vertex operator algebras.\pause\\[2ex]

Fusion categories encompass the more general theory, with applications to operator algebras and categorical representation theory.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
\frametitle{Motivation IV}
Currently, there are four ``primitive sources'' of fusion categories (\cite{EIP24}).\pause\\[1.5ex]
\begin{itemize}[<+->]
\item \textcolor{structure}{Finite groups}: $\textcat{Vec}_G^\omega$.%, for $G$ a finite group and $\omega$ a $3$-cocycle.
\item \textcolor{structure}{Quantum groups}: %(semisimple quotients of) 
$\overline{\textcat{Tilt}(U_q^L(\mathfrak{g}))}$.%, for $U_q(\mathfrak{g})$ a Drinfeld-Jimbo quantum group at a root of unity $q$.
\item \textcolor{structure}{Quadratic extensions}: ``quadratic extensions'' of $\textcat{Vec}_G$.%finite groups; that is, $\textcat{Vec}_G$ with an additional non-invertible simple.
\item \textcolor{structure}{Extended Haagerup}: four Morita equivalent categories not known to arise from the previous three sources.\\[2ex]
\end{itemize}\pause[\thebeamerpauses]
Every \textcolor{structure}{known} fusion category arises from these families under elementary constructions (such as Deligne tensor products, (de-)equivariantization, Morita equivalence and graded extensions).
\end{frame}

%\begin{frame}
%\frametitle{Motivation III}
%As alluded to previously, fusion categories appear frequently in physics.\\[2ex]

%1. There is a bijective correspondence between certain equivalence classes of ``sufficiently finite'' %finite index, finite depth, hyperfinite
%subfactors and certain %Morita
%equivalence classes of unitary fusion categories.\\[2ex]

%2. There is a bijective correspondence between ``sufficiently nice'' 3D TQFTs and certain equivalence classes of non-degenerate ribbon fusion categories.\\[2ex]

%3. These so-called {\em modular tensor categories} are also used to model anyons in the realm of topological quantum computing.
%\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
\frametitle{Motivation V}
The fusion categories coming from finite groups and quantum groups are well-understood. The extended Haagerup fusion categories form just one class. This only leaves \textcolor{structure}{quadratic categories}, which will be my focus today.\pause\\[2ex]

\textcolor{structure}{Quadratic fusion categories} contain $\textcat{Vec}_G$, but have two orbits under the $G$-action rather than one. An important family of quadratic categories are the \textcolor{structure}{near-groups}:%\\[2ex]
%A \textcolor{structure}{near-group fusion category} is one whose simple objects are elements of a finite group together with a single non-invertible simple. In other words, we take $\textcat{Vec}_G$ and add a new simple object $X$ such that
\[
g \otimes h \cong gh,
\]
\[
g \otimes X \cong X \cong X \otimes g,
\]
\[
X \otimes X \cong \bigoplus_{g \in G}g \oplus X^{\oplus m}.
\]\pause
%for some multiplicity parameter $m$.\\[2ex]

In this talk, I would like to address the problem of classifying \textcolor{structure}{near-group fusion categories}, beginning with Izumi's work in the unitary setting.
\end{frame}

\section{Endomorphisms of Cuntz Algebras}

\begin{frame}
\noindent\centerline{\LARGE\textcolor{structure}{\underline{Endomorphisms of Cuntz Algebras}}}
\end{frame}

%\begin{frame}
%Upshot, what is Cuntz algebra, Izumi's Cuntz algebra realization, etc.
%A result of Hayashi and Yamagami shows that \textcolor{structure}{every unitary fusion category} embeds into $\textup{End}(M)$, for $M$ a hyperfinite type III$_1$ factor (\cite{HY00}). Popa's famous classification theorem for subfactors (\cite{Pop95}) then provides a compatible notion of equivalence.\\[2ex]

%In \cite{Izu93} and \cite{Izu01}, Izumi showed that unitary near-group categories realized in $\textup{End}(M)$ could be reconstructed from \textcolor{structure}{Cuntz algebra endomorphisms}.\\[2ex]

%This led to a full classification of unitary near-groups in \cite{Izu17}.
%\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
An important result of Hayashi and Yamagami showed that \textcolor{structure}{every unitary fusion category} embeds into $\textup{End}(M)$, for $M$ a hyperfinite type III$_1$ factor (\cite{HY00}). Popa's famous classification theorem for subfactors (\cite{Pop95}) then provides a compatible notion of equivalence.\pause\\[2ex]

In \cite{Izu93} and \cite{Izu01}, Izumi showed that unitary near-group categories realized in $\textup{End}(M)$, for $M$ a hyperfinite type III$_1$ factor, could be reconstructed from \textcolor{structure}{Cuntz algebra endomorphisms}.\pause\\[2ex]

A \textcolor{structure}{Cuntz algebra} is a universal $C^*$-algebra $\mathcal{O}_n$ generated by a set of isometries $\{S_i\}_{i=1}^n$ satisfying
\[
\sum_{i=1}^n S_iS_i^* = 1.
\]
Since this implies $S_i^*S_j = \delta_{i,j}$ automatically, it follows that Cuntz algebras capture exactly the notion of \textcolor{structure}{direct sums} in the unitary setting.\\[2ex]
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
By \cite{HY00}, given a \textcolor{structure}{type $(G, m)$ near-group} $\mathcal{C}$, there are $\rho \in \textup{End}(M)$ and $\alpha : G \to \textup{Aut}(M)$ satisfying our fusion rules:\pause
\[\begin{array}{ccc}
g \otimes h \cong gh&\rightsquigarrow&\alpha_g(\alpha_h(x)) = \alpha_{gh}(x);\pause\\[2ex]
g \otimes X \cong X \cong X \otimes g&\rightsquigarrow&\begin{matrix*}[c]\alpha_g(\rho(x)) = \rho(x),\\[1ex]\rho(\alpha_g(x)) = U(g)\rho(x)U(g)^{-1};\end{matrix*}\pause\\[4ex]
\displaystyle X \otimes X \cong \bigoplus_{g \in G}g \oplus X^{\oplus m}&\rightsquigarrow&\rho(\rho(x)) = \displaystyle\sum_{g \in G}{S_g\alpha_g(x)S_g^*} + \sum_{i=1}^m{T_i\rho(x)T_i^*}.
\end{array}\]
These $S_g$ and $T_i$ are the inclusion maps for the summands $\alpha_g$ and $\rho$ inside $\rho \otimes \rho$, and hence naturally generate a Cuntz subalgebra $\mathcal{O}_{\abs{G}+m}$ of $M$.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
In \cite{Izu17}, Izumi showed that $\rho$ and $\alpha_g$ are \textcolor{structure}{polynomials in the Cuntz algebra generators}, and moreover that the \textcolor{structure}{$6j$-symbols} of $\mathcal{C}$ are completely determined by\\[1.5ex]
\begin{itemize}
\item a finite-dimensional Hilbert space $\mathcal{K} \coloneqq \textup{Hom}(\rho, \rho^2)$,\\[2ex]
\item two antilinear isometries $j_1, j_2 \in \textup{End}(\mathcal{K})$,\\[2ex]
\item a unitary representation $V : G \to \textup{End}(\mathcal{K})$,\\[2ex]
\item a unitary representation $U_\mathcal{K} : G \to \textup{End}(\mathcal{K})$,\\[2ex]
\item a symmetric bicharacter $\chi : G \times G \to \mathbb{T}$,\\[2ex]
\item a linear map $l : \mathcal{K} \to \mathcal{B}(\mathcal{K}, \mathcal{K} \otimes \mathcal{K})$.\\[2ex]
\end{itemize}
He then deduced necessary and sufficient conditions for this data to reproduce $\mathcal{C}$, as well as a compatible notion of equivalence.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
The end result of this approach was a system of polynomial equations such that there exists a \textcolor{structure}{bijective correspondence} between equivalence classes of solutions and equivalence classes of unitary near-group categories.\\[2ex]

This classification also simplifies significantly in certain circumstances. In particular, there are strong restrictions on what $m$ can be.\pause\\[2ex]

If \textcolor{structure}{$\dim(X)$ is rational}, then $\dim(X)$ is necessarily an integer. This situation is group-theoretical and fairly well-understood.\pause\\[2ex]

If \textcolor{structure}{$\dim(X)$ is irrational}, then $G$ is Abelian and $m = k\abs{G}$ for $k \in \mathbb{N}$. When $k = 1$, the data needed for the classification reduces to just\\[0.5ex]
\begin{itemize}
\item two functions $a, b : G \to \mathbb{T}$,\\[1ex]
\item a scalar $c \in \mathbb{T}$\\[1ex]
\end{itemize}
satisfying various polynomial equations.\pause\\[2ex]

Let's see a couple of very simple examples.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
\begin{example}
When $G = \{0\}$,\\
\begin{itemize}
\item $a$ must be given by $a(0) = 1$;\\
\item $b$ must be given by $b(0) = -1/\dim(X)$;\\
\item $c$ must be given by $c = 1$.
\end{itemize}
Thus there is one unitary type $(\{0\}, 1)$ near-group. This is the \textcolor{structure}{Fibonacci category} and corresponds to the even part of the type $A_4$ subfactor.\pause
\end{example}
\noindent\\[-1ex]
\begin{example}
When $G = \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$,\\
\begin{itemize}
\item $a$ must be given by $a(0) = 1$ and $a(1) = \pm i$;\\
\item $b$ must be given by $b(0) = -1/\dim(X)$ and $b(1) = (1 - a(1))/2$;\\
\item $c$ must be given by $c = \frac{1 - \csqrt{3} + a(1)(1 + \csqrt{3})}{2\csqrt{2}}$.\\
\end{itemize}
Thus there are two unitary type $(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}, 2)$ near-groups. These are adjoint subcategories of exceptional quantum subgroups of Temperley--Lieb--Jones.
\end{example}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
This is a significant improvement over na\"{i}vely determining the $6j$-symbols!\\[2ex]

For instance, the space $\textup{Hom}_\mathcal{C}((X \otimes X) \otimes X, X \otimes (X \otimes X))$ alone has dimension
\[
m^4 + 3m^2\abs{G} + \abs{G}^2.
\]
Meanwhile, in the $m = \abs{G}$ case, the data needed for Izumi's classification is only $2m + 1$.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
There is also an interesting observation we can make about these two examples. The near-group fusion rules imply that
\[
\dim(X) = \frac{m \pm \csqrt{m^2 + 4n}}{2}.
\]
In the unitary setting, this must be positive, so there is only really one choice. In the non-unitary setting, this restriction is lifted.\pause\\[2ex]

If we work with the Galois conjugates of the dimension, we will find that there {\em are} choices for $(a, b, c)$ that satisfy all but two of our polynomial equations, and these correspond to non-unitary near-groups!\\[2ex]

This is the first piece of evidence that we could expect to find a non-unitary version of Izumi's classification.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
The second piece of evidence comes from Evans and Gannon.\\[2ex]

Izumi's approach of realizing unitary fusion categories via Cuntz algebra endomorphisms, together with the work of Phillips on non-unitary analogues of Cuntz algebras (\cite{Phi12}), inspired Evans and Gannon to devise a non-unitary version of this classification for \textcolor{structure}{Haagerup--Izumi fusion categories} (\cite{EG17}).\\[2ex]

In particular, they were able to realize certain non-unitary Haagerup--Izumi categories via \textcolor{structure}{Leavitt algebra endomorphisms}.\pause\\[2ex]

We would like to see if this approach can give us new examples of non-unitary near-groups. In general, the non-unitary setting is much more mysterious than the unitary setting!
\end{frame}

\section{The Non-Unitary Setting: Leavitt Algebras}

\begin{frame}
\noindent\centerline{\LARGE\textcolor{structure}{\underline{The Non-Unitary Setting: Leavitt Algebras}}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
For us, a \textcolor{structure}{Leavitt algebra} will be a universal $\mathbbm{k}$-algebra $\mathcal{L}_n$ with generators $\{s_i\}_{i=1}^n \sqcup \{s_i'\}_{i=1}^n$ satisfying
\[
\sum_{i=1}^n s_is_i' = 1\qquad\text{and}\qquad s_i's_j = \delta_{i,j}.
\]
This is a straightforward generalization of the Cuntz algebra to the non-unitary setting.\pause\\[2ex]

It is \textcolor{structure}{not known} whether all non-unitary fusion categories may be realized as categories of endomorphisms. In the unitary setting this result comes from subfactor theory, which we no longer have access to.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
So far, what we have shown is as follows. Given a type $(G, m)$ near-group that admits a realization via algebra endomorphisms, $\alpha_g$ and $\rho$ will restrict to endomorphisms of a Leavitt algebra $\mathcal{L}_{\abs{G}+m}$. Moreover, the $6j$-symbols are completely determined by\\[1.5ex]
\begin{itemize}
\item finite-dimensional spaces $\mathcal{K} \coloneqq \textup{Hom}(\rho, \rho^2)$ \textcolor{structure}{and $\mathcal{K}' \coloneqq \textup{Hom}(\rho^2, \rho)$},\\[2ex]
\item two morphisms $j_1', j_2' \in \textup{Hom}(\mathcal{K}, \mathcal{K}')$,\\[2ex]
\item \textcolor{structure}{an automorphism $\varphi \in \textup{Aut}(\mathcal{K})$},\\[2ex]
\item a representation $V : G \to \textup{End}(\mathcal{K})$,\\[2ex]
\item a representation $U_\mathcal{K} : G \to \textup{End}(\mathcal{K})$,\\[2ex]
\item a symmetric bicharacter $\chi : G \times G \to \mathbbm{k}^\times$,\\[2ex]
\item two linear maps $l : \mathcal{K} \to \mathcal{K}^2\mathcal{K}'$ \textcolor{structure}{and $l' : \mathcal{K}' \to \mathcal{K}(\mathcal{K}')^2$}.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
The group-theoretic component of the project has essentially been completed.\pause\\[2ex]

The goal moving forward is to focus on the case when $\dim(X)$ is irrational, beginning with $m = \abs{G}$. Izumi's unitary classification allows us to make some educated guesses as to what the constraints on our data could look like.\pause\\[2ex]

The main limitation is, of course, that there could perhaps be non-unitary near-groups that escape this kind of realization. I'm very curious about results in this direction!
\end{frame}
